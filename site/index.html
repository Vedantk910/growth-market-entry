How I built it.

The arc from a fuzzy “should we enter?” to a tool a client can re-run.

  1. Frame one decision.“Should a last-mile operator enter e-commerce fulfillment?” reduced to three strategies priced on one yardstick — USD/parcel net contribution: enter in-house, partner, or decline.
  2. Source only public numbers, each tagged.Top-down: Menzies Parcel Economy (124.9B US parcels, EST) anchored by the filed USPS commercial-package figure (53.5B, 2023 — CONFIRMED). Bottom-up: USPS, UPS and RRD filings, plus RRD's +41% fulfillment volume growth as the adjacent-market entry case. No figure on this page is untagged.
  3. Model with closed forms first.The flip volume is one line of algebra, V* = F/(p − cv); the 5-year PV break-even is a ratio of discounted annuity sums, pinned against a binary-search sweep to 1e-6. No black-box numerics anywhere on the path a client would re-run.
  4. Prove it, not just claim it.33 offline pytest cases: a closed-form-vs-numeric pin, a scale/decay invariance check, and a sensitivity check on the pivot inputs per module. Zero network, deterministic, one-command re-run.
  5. Answer the hostile question.An offline cross-check proves the trigger is invariant to every market-size factor — halve the SAM and the flip is bit-identical — while every pivot input moves it. That ablation is the answer to “your market number is a range — which number does the decision rest on?”
  6. Make it runnable and feelable.python notebooks/run_notebook.py regenerates the decision numbers; GitHub Actions runs the suite on every push; this page re-implements the same closed forms in JS and a Node harness pins the two languages to the same bits.

Three attacks, answered with a number.

What I'd get asked in a strategy interview — and the defence that holds.

Attack 1

“Your market number is a range — which one does the decision actually rest on?”

Not the SAM. The decision rests on the flip F/(p−cv) and the decline rate R − floor — closed forms in cost and the partner rate. The repo's offline cross-check proves it: halve the SAM and the trigger is bit-identical; the SAM only sets headroom (54× the flip at baseline).

Attack 2

“The partner rate and hub cost are two numbers you picked — the whole thing is one parameter.”

Two inputs, isolated by the formula: the client sees exactly how much each is worth, and the cost × rate surface moves the trigger continuously across the band — that's the re-runnable part. Build / partner / decline are priced on one yardstick, not three gut calls.

Attack 3

“RRD's $223/parcel says nothing about last-mile cost — your bottom-up bench is broken.”

It's tagged, and the tag does the work: the per-parcel figures are derived (revenue ÷ volume), not filed. RRD anchors the fulfillment margin and the adjacent-entry question; it does not anchor delivery unit cost — which is why the pivot is the committee's cost-to-serve, not the filing.
